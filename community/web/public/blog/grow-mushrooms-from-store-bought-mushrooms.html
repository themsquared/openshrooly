Can you grow mushrooms from store-bought mushrooms?

By Shroom Labs. Published November 6, 2026.

Yes for oysters, rarely for grocery button or cremini. Fresh oyster (Pleurotus) stem butts often grow onto damp cardboard or sterile agar, and University of Florida extension even recommends starting a mother culture from market oysters. Grain spawn from that culture colonizes in about 10 to 20 days at 23 °C (73 °F). Button, cremini and portobello are Agaricus bisporus. They need Phase I and Phase II compost plus a casing layer, and U.S. grocery packs are mostly commercial hybrids that have already spent days in the cold chain. Cloning them at home almost never turns into a crop.

Short answer

You can clone some grocery mushrooms. You cannot plant a mushroom like a seed and expect a new cluster. What works is living mycelium still inside fresh tissue. Tear or cut that tissue onto a food source it can colonize, then grow it out into spawn and a fruiting substrate.

Pearl oyster (Pleurotus ostreatus) is the species that makes this practical at home. Cornell Small Farms lists oysters among the easiest edible mushrooms to grow and notes they run on straw, sawdust, shredded paper, coffee grounds and similar plant waste. Button / cremini (Agaricus bisporus) is a different story. Our profile tags it advanced and compost-only, and commercial California production still runs Phase I fermentation, a 10 to 14 day Phase II pasteurization and conditioning cycle, then casing before pins form.

UF/IFAS (SS663) says oyster mushrooms can be cloned from the basidiocarp onto agar, and for isolation it recommends market oysters so you start with an edible cultivated strain. Stamets describes stem butts in Mycelium Running as a low-tech clone: many saprophytes with living basal mycelium will regrow when the butt is pressed into damp cardboard or wood chips.

Buttons fail for substrate reasons more than cloning theater. UC ANR notes that U.S. production is dominated by Agaricus hybrids grown as secondary decomposers on composted materials, not on raw straw. Ammonia left after Phase I is inhibitory and lethal to mushroom growth until Phase II removes it. Even a clean tissue culture has nowhere useful to go without that compost and a non-nutritive casing on top.

Tissue cloning vs stem butt on cardboard

Two common methods do different jobs.

Tissue on agar is the clean route. UF/IFAS: pull the oyster cap apart to expose the inside uncontaminated tissue, then move tiny pieces with sterile tweezers onto potato dextrose agar or a similar medium, ideally in a still-air box or flow hood. When white hyphae grow out without mold or bacteria, subculture the leading edge. That mother culture inoculates sterilized grain. Incubate at 23 °C (73 °F) in the dark until the grain is white, about 10 to 20 days. Store finished spawn at 4 °C (39 °F) for non-tropical Pleurotus strains, and subculture mother plates every 6 to 12 months.

Stem butt on cardboard skips sterile media. You use the dense base of the stem, where white fuzz (mycelium) often still shows, as the inoculum, and boiled corrugated cardboard as a low-nutrient first food. Cellulose-heavy cardboard favors aggressive wood and straw eaters like oysters and gives molds less to live on than rich grain. It is less reliable than agar, and yields from cardboard spawn are lower than from grain, but it needs no pressure cooker.

Use tissue cloning when you want a stable culture and grain spawn. Use stem butts when you want a cheap first try from tonight's oysters.

Stem butt cloning onto cardboard

  1. Pick the freshest oyster cluster you can find at the store. Caps should be firm, not slimy or darkened. Stamets stresses fresh tissue for any clone. Work the same day, or keep the pack refrigerated and use it within about two days.
  2. Tear plain brown corrugated cardboard into pieces that fit a clean plastic container. Avoid heavy ink where you can. Cover the pieces with boiling water, lid on, and let them cool to room temperature. Drain until a squeeze leaves them damp, not dripping.
  3. Melt a few small air holes in the lid with a heated nail. Wash your hands and wipe the work surface.
  4. Eat the caps. Tear (do not slice) the stem bases where the cluster was joined. Keep any white fuzz on the butts. Tearing exposes clean inner cells instead of dragging surface contaminants through the tissue the way a knife can.
  5. Lay a layer of damp cardboard in the container, spread the stem butts in one even layer, then cover with more cardboard. Pack enough butts that mycelium has a chance to meet and outcompete whatever else is on the board.
  6. Close the lid and keep the container dark at about 20 to 24 °C (68 to 75 °F). Check after about 10 to 14 days, and allow up to about 3 weeks before judging it.
  7. Success is white, thread-like mycelium running from the butts across the cardboard until the pieces bind together and smell earthy. Failure is green, black, orange or slimy patches, or a sour smell. Contaminated cardboard spawn is not salvageable. Throw the whole container out and start over with fresher mushrooms.

When the cardboard is mostly white, break it up into pasteurized straw, more boiled cardboard, or a fresh coffee-and-straw mix. Our coffee grounds guide and oyster kit day-by-day cover the next stages. Plan dates with the grow timeline calculator.

Why grocery mushrooms often fail

Age and the cold chain. Mycelium in a stem butt is living tissue. Days on a truck and under a misted case leave less vigor than a cluster picked that morning. Older packs get soft, wet and colonized by bacteria before you open them.

Species biology. Oysters are primary decomposers that run on pasteurized plant waste. Buttons are secondary decomposers that expect a finished compost. Cornell's urban mushroom sheet puts oysters on coffee grounds, straw and shredded paper. It does not put Agaricus there. Our button / cremini profile needs a spawn run of about 16 days on compost, then casing and pinning for about 8 days, then fruiting for about 12 days, with little to no light. Our pearl oyster profile colonizes about 14 days at 24 °C (75 °F), pins about 5 days at 18 °C (64 °F), and fruits about 10 days at 16 °C (61 °F) with blue light and heavy fresh air.

Cultivar and handling. UC ANR reports that Agaricus hybrids dominate U.S. commercial production, with white, off-white, cream and brown lines selected for farm rooms, not for cardboard buckets. Grocery oysters are usually vigorous cultivated Pleurotus strains, which is why UF/IFAS is comfortable starting a mother culture from a market pack.

Contamination. Cardboard and coffee methods are not sterile. Green mold (Trichoderma) and bacteria win when the butts are old, the cardboard is too wet, or the oyster share is too thin. See the contamination guide for what those failures look like.

Honest odds

Expect oysters to be worth trying and buttons not to be. With a firm, same-day oyster cluster, stem butt cardboard often colonizes; tissue on agar is more consistent if you can keep the plate clean. Either way you still need a pasteurized fruiting substrate and the humidity, fresh air and light in the oyster profile. A limp week-old pack is a kitchen experiment that usually molds.

For button, cremini or portobello from the store, treat success as unlikely. Even a clean agar plate needs Phase II compost free of ammonia, a casing layer, and cool humid pinning. That is farm infrastructure, not a windowsill project. If you want those mushrooms at home, get spawn and ready compost made for Agaricus, or start with oysters instead. FAQ

Can you grow mushrooms from store-bought mushrooms?

Yes for fresh oysters, by cloning stem butts or inner tissue, not by planting the mushroom whole. Grocery button and cremini almost never become a home crop because they need compost and casing.

Do you need agar to clone oyster mushrooms?

No. Agar gives a cleaner mother culture and better grain spawn. Stem butts on boiled cardboard are the no-lab path Stamets describes for saprophytes with living basal mycelium.

How long does cardboard spawn take?

Check around 10 to 14 days and allow up to about 3 weeks. White mycelium binding the cardboard is success. Colored mold or a sour smell means discard it.

Why won't grocery button mushrooms grow on cardboard or coffee grounds?

Agaricus bisporus is a secondary decomposer. Commercial production uses Phase I and a 10 to 14 day Phase II compost cycle, then a casing layer to force pins. Cardboard and coffee suit primary decomposers like oysters.

Are grocery oyster mushrooms safe to clone?

UF/IFAS recommends cultivated market oysters for mother culture isolation so you start with a known edible Pleurotus strain. Still verify you bought cultivated oysters, not a wild lookalike, and discard any culture that grows mold.

Is cloning the same as growing from spores?

No. A tissue or stem butt clone keeps the genetics of that mushroom. Spores recombine and give new individuals. Cloning is how you copy a grocery oyster that already fruited well.
