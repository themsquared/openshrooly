When to harvest mushrooms, species by species

By Mike Moore. Published November 27, 2026.

Harvest by shape, not size. Most cap mushrooms are ready just before the cap edge flattens out or the veil under the cap tears. Pick oyster clusters while the cap edges still curl under or are just starting to flatten, usually 5 to 10 days after the block goes into fruiting. Pick shiitake when the cap is 50 to 75% open and the gill edges are still rolled under. Cut lion's mane while the spines are still white, before any yellow shows. Our dense head profile cuts it 7 to 12 days after the head appears. Button and cremini go before the veil stretches open. Twist or cut at the base, clean up the stubs, and expect the next flush about 10 days later for oysters and about 7 to 14 days later for most blocks.

How do you know when a mushroom is ready to harvest?

Look at the edge of the cap, not the size of the mushroom. As a mushroom matures, the cap opens. On oysters the edge goes from curled under, to flat, to flipped up. On shiitake, button, chestnut and wine cap a thin veil connects the cap edge to the stem, then stretches and tears. Pick just before that last step. Penn State says it plainly for button mushrooms: maturity is judged by how far the veil is stretched, not by how large the mushroom is, so ready mushrooms can be big or small (Penn State, Six Steps to Mushroom Farming).

Timing is fast. Ohio State notes oyster mushrooms expand rapidly over a couple of days (Ohio State), so check twice a day once the pins turn into small caps. A cluster that looks a day early in the morning can be past its best by the next evening.

Picking on time also keeps spores down. Mushrooms left to open flat drop heavy spore loads, and NCAT tells growers to harvest promptly for that reason (NCAT ATTRA 2026). A layer of fine powder on the block or the chamber floor is a sign you waited too long.

Oyster mushrooms: pearl, blue, black, pink and golden

Pick oysters as a whole cluster when the cap edges are still curled under or just starting to flatten. UC ANR puts the oyster harvest stage directly before the curled margin rises to flat (UC ANR). The SARE funded oyster booklet says the same: harvest before the cap edges flatten out completely or flip up, usually 5 to 10 days after the substrate goes into fruiting conditions (Crosby 2016). Ohio State adds that picking before the caps curl gives a less tough mushroom (Ohio State).

Each oyster has its own tell:

  • Pearl oyster. Our pearl oyster profile says harvest when caps flatten and the edges just begin to upturn. For small restaurant style clusters, the pearl oyster baby clusters profile picks earlier, while the edges still roll under.
  • Blue oyster. Cap color depends on temperature and on the stage you pick, from blue to near white (Crosby 2016). Pick early for the darkest blue.
  • Black oyster. Our black oyster profile notes the caps start near black and lighten to slate grey as they open. Harvest before the edges flatten.
  • Pink oyster. The fastest one. Ohio State says it fruits in just 7 to 10 days at 85 to 90 °F (29 to 32 °C) (Ohio State). Our pink profiles say the color fades as the fruits mature, so pick as soon as the caps open and before the edges turn up.
  • Golden oyster. Ohio State calls it delicate and quick to show damage (Ohio State). Our golden oyster deep yellow profile says harvest before the caps turn up at the edge.

Cut the whole cluster off at the base with a clean knife or scissors, as Ohio State recommends (Ohio State), or hold the base and twist it off. Taking the cluster in one piece is easier on the block than picking caps one at a time, and the small caps in a cluster are fine to eat.

King oyster

King oyster is grown for the stem, so the sign is different. Our king oyster profile lets the stems lengthen before the caps flare. Once the cap starts to open wide and flatten, the stem stops getting better. The king oyster thick stem profile pulls extra pins early so one or two stems get all the food. Cut each stem at the base. Kings keep better than other oysters, up to a week in the fridge per Ohio State (Ohio State).

Lion's mane

Lion's mane has no cap, so read the spines. Our lion's mane profile says harvest when the spines have lengthened but before they yellow. If you want a firm, crab like texture, our lion's mane dense head profile cuts earlier, 7 to 12 days after the head appears, while the spines are still short. Either way, white is the rule. Minnesota DNR notes lion's mane is best eaten white and that yellowing means it is aging and may taste bitter. Cut it at the base with a long knife (Minnesota DNR).

A faint pink blush in a cool room is harmless, per the dense head profile. Pink, yellow and brown each mean something different. Lion's mane turning pink, yellow or brown covers all three.

Shiitake

Ohio State Extension says to pick shiitake when the cap is about 50 to 75% open, while the gill edges are still rolled under the cap. Picking at 100% open shortens shelf life, delays the next flush and invites pests (Ohio State Extension 2022). For thick, rounded caps, our shiitake donko profile picks under 70% open.

Grab the lower part of the stem and pull with a slight twist, or cut it as close to the block as you can with a sharp knife or scissors. Touch only the stem. Bruised caps and gills discolor fast (Ohio State Extension 2022).

Button and cremini

Button and cremini are the same species. Pick before the veil under the cap stretches open. Penn State says North American shoppers want closed, tight caps, while open browns are sold as portobello (Penn State, Six Steps to Mushroom Farming). Pick each one by hand and trim the base. Our button and cremini profile grows on compost with a casing layer, which is a different setup from a sawdust block.

Chestnut, beech shimeji and wine cap

  • Chestnut. Our chestnut long stem profile says pick before the veil tears. Cut the cluster at the base. See the chestnut profile for the standard grow.
  • Beech shimeji. Penn State describes commercial growers removing the entire cluster when the mushrooms are mature (Penn State). Take the whole bouquet at once rather than picking caps.
  • Wine cap. University of Missouri says to harvest wine caps just before the caps break free from the ring around the stem (University of Missouri). Cornell notes the red brown cap fades lighter and the gills darken as it matures, so a darker cap and lighter gills mean a younger mushroom (Cornell). Wine cap is mainly an outdoor bed mushroom. NCAT describes outdoor wine cap beds giving steady harvests through the warm season (NCAT ATTRA 2026). Our wine cap profile is a climate approximation for indoor tries.

How to harvest a flush

  1. Check the chamber twice a day once you see small caps. Read the cap edge, veil or spines, not the size.
  2. Wash your hands or wear clean gloves. Use a clean, sharp knife or scissors.
  3. Hold the cluster or stem at the base, not the cap.
  4. Twist it off with a slight turn, or cut it as close to the block as you can.
  5. Take the whole cluster at once for oysters, beech and lion's mane.
  6. Pick off stem stubs and bits left on the block so they do not rot.
  7. Get the harvest into a paper bag in the fridge right away.
  8. Rest the block in the same damp conditions, then start the next flush.

Twist or cut?

Both work. Ohio State gives both options for shiitake: a slight twist from the lower stem, or a cut close to the surface with a knife or scissors (Ohio State Extension 2022). University of Kentucky's shiitake and oyster guide says the same: cut or twist them off at the base of the stem (University of Kentucky).

Cut when a twist would rip up the block, as with a big oyster cluster or a lion's mane head. Twist when the stem pops off clean, as with shiitake and button. Either way, clean up what is left. A stub left on the block rots and invites mold. Green mold on mushroom substrate covers what to do if you see it.

How long until the next flush?

Most blocks give more than one flush, but each one is smaller. NCAT says many systems give one to three flushes and suggests a rest of roughly 7 to 14 days between them, with the block kept damp (NCAT ATTRA 2026).

  • Oysters. UC ANR spaces oyster flushes about 10 days apart (UC ANR).
  • Shiitake. Rest the block before soaking it again. In a 2024 study, blocks rested two weeks in the dark at 22 °C (72 °F) and 60% humidity, then got another 24 hour ice soak (El Sebaaly et al. 2024). Penn State says mushrooms are ready about 9 to 11 days after soaking (Penn State). UC ANR counts up to 4 indoor flushes depending on the soaking schedule (UC ANR).
  • Button and cremini. Flushes run on a 7 to 10 day cycle. Picking the mature mushrooms removes an inhibitor so the next flush moves forward (Penn State, Six Steps to Mushroom Farming).
  • Beech shimeji. Commercial bottle growers harvest only one flush before replacing the substrate (Penn State). A home block may give a small second one, but do not count on it.

If the second flush stalls, the block may be dry or spent. Mushroom grow kit not growing walks through it. When a block is done, start a new grow from /grow.

FAQ

How do you know when oyster mushrooms are ready to harvest?

When the cap edges are still curled under or are just starting to flatten. Do not wait for the edges to flip up. That is usually 5 to 10 days after the block goes into fruiting conditions, and oysters can go from early to late in about a day.

When do you harvest lion's mane?

When the spines have grown long but are still white, with no yellow. For a firmer texture, cut earlier, about 7 to 12 days after the head appears. Cut it at the base with a knife.

When do you harvest shiitake?

When the cap is about 50 to 75% open and the gill edges are still rolled under. Waiting until fully open shortens shelf life and delays the next flush.

Should you twist or cut mushrooms?

Either. Twist when the stem pops off cleanly, as with shiitake and button. Cut with a clean knife or scissors when twisting would tear the block, as with oyster clusters and lion's mane.

What happens if you harvest too late?

The caps flatten or flip up, the texture gets tougher, lion's mane turns yellow and bitter, and the mushrooms drop spores everywhere. They are usually still edible, just not as good, and they keep for less time.

Can you harvest mushrooms too early?

Yes, but you only lose size. Small, tight mushrooms are often the best eating. The pearl oyster baby clusters profile picks early on purpose.

How long between mushroom flushes?

Most blocks need a rest of about 7 to 14 days. Oysters come back in about 10 days. Shiitake blocks rest about two weeks, then need another cold soak, and fruit about 9 to 11 days after it.

Do you harvest the whole cluster at once?

For oysters, beech and lion's mane, yes. Take the cluster in one piece at the base. Shiitake, button and wine cap are picked one at a time as each is ready.
