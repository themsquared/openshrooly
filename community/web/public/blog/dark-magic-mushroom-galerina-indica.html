What is the dark magic mushroom?

By Mike Moore. Published November 17, 2026.

The dark magic mushroom is Galerina indica, a thumbnail-sized brown mushroom found on a rotting, moss-covered palm trunk in a Florida wetland. A University of Florida team reported in Current Biology on 2026-11-11 that it makes both psilocybin, the psychedelic compound in magic mushrooms, and amatoxins, the toxins that make death caps deadly. It is the first mushroom known to make both. Amatoxins damage the liver, symptoms can start 6 to 24 hours after eating and then seem to fade, and no wild mushroom should be eaten on a guess. If someone has eaten an unknown wild mushroom, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 right away, or 911 if they are very sick.

Legal status: Legal status checked 2026-11-17 against the cited federal source. Psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule I under federal law. This post is science and safety information, not legal advice, and it is not a guide to finding, growing or eating any mushroom. Psilocybin mushrooms are illegal to grow, possess, or sell in most countries and under US federal law. This information is published for educational, research and harm-reduction purposes only. Short answer

Until this month, mushrooms fell into two camps. Some make psilocybin and some make amatoxins, and nobody had found one that makes both. Galerina indica does. Researchers at the University of Florida nicknamed it the "dark magic mushroom," and their paper came out in Current Biology on 2026-11-11.

This post is science and safety information. It covers what the researchers actually reported, why the combination matters to anyone who picks wild mushrooms, and what to do if someone eats the wrong one. It is not a guide to finding, growing or eating anything. Shroom Labs does not sell spores, cultures or psilocybin products.

What is the dark magic mushroom?

It is a small brown mushroom in the genus Galerina, the "little brown mushroom" group. The species is Galerina indica. The largest ones the team found stood about 0.2 inches (0.5 cm) tall, with caps about 0.1 inches (0.25 cm) wide, so a cluster fits on a thumbnail.

Two things made it worth a second look. Some Galerina species were already known to make amatoxins. And this one turned blue when handled, which is a trait seen in some psilocybin mushrooms. The team tested for both compound families and found both.

The word "magic" is the news hook. The word "dark" is the point: the same mushroom carries a toxin that can destroy a liver.

Who found it, and where?

According to the University of Florida, Andrew Kunik found the mushroom while documenting fungi at the UF/IFAS Ordway Swisher Biological Station. Science News reports it was in 2025, on a decaying, moss-covered palm trunk in a central Florida wetland. Kunik was a student researcher in the lab of Matthew E. Smith, a plant pathologist who is also Florida's state mycologist.

The paper's team came from the University of Florida, the University of Utah, Ohio State University and the University of California, Berkeley. The preprint lists five Galerina specimens collected in the southeastern United States. Three were Galerina indica. Two were other Galerina that the team could not match to a named species.

Science News notes that Galerina indica had not been reported in Florida before, and that earlier researchers had described how it looks and found DNA markers for it, but nobody had mapped its chemistry or its genes.

How did researchers know it has both compounds?

They used mass spectrometry, which measures the mass of the molecules in a tissue sample, and then they checked the genes. Here is what the open preprint says, in plain terms.

  • Chemistry. Tiny samples of dried mushroom tissue, 1 to 2 milligrams each, were analyzed by tandem mass spectrometry. The team ran authenticated standards for comparison, and ran two control mushrooms alongside: an Amanita known to make amatoxins but not psilocybin, and a polypore expected to make none of the compounds. The controls behaved as expected.
  • Result. All three Galerina indica collections contained psilocybin and psilocin plus two amatoxins. Of the other two Galerina, one had psilocybin and psilocin but no detectable amatoxins, and the other had neither.
  • Genes. A genome analysis found the gene sets that build both kinds of compound. Psilocybin comes from a compact five-gene cluster. Amatoxins come from a different gene system.
  • Open data. The raw mass spectra are public in the MassIVE repository under accession MSV000101888.

One caution about sources. The method details above come from the open bioRxiv preprint posted in July 2026, because I could not open the Current Biology page. The journal version is the version of record, so check it if a number matters to you.

Why is a mushroom with psilocybin and amatoxins so dangerous?

Because one effect hides the other. Psilocybin is the reason people go looking for magic mushrooms. Amatoxins are a delayed poison. A person who eats an unidentified little brown mushroom expecting a psychedelic effect could be taking in a liver toxin, and the first warning may come hours later.

The researchers' own words are blunt. Smith said these mushrooms are small and brown like hundreds of other species, and that they are hard to tell apart "even for a trained expert." Mycologist Anne Pringle, who was not part of the study, told Science News that when people ask "can I eat it?" about a new mushroom, "this paper confirms that, in fact, you should be uncomfortable."

I did not find any report of a human poisoning from Galerina indica in the sources I read. The point is not that people are being poisoned by this species. The point is that the lines people rely on, "magic mushrooms are one thing and poisonous mushrooms are another," do not hold.

How do amatoxins poison people?

Amatoxins are ring-shaped peptides. According to the preprint, they shut down an enzyme called RNA polymerase II, which cells need to make proteins. Liver cells take up amatoxins from the gut, and that is why the liver is hit hardest.

The CDC describes the pattern in three phases:

  1. Hours 6 and later. Delayed abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea. Liver tests can still look normal.
  2. Hours 12 to 36. Lab signs of liver injury, trouble with blood clotting and kidney injury.
  3. Days 2 to 6. Worsening liver function that can progress to liver and kidney failure. Some patients need a liver transplant.

The CDC adds that amatoxins are not destroyed by cooking or other food preparation, and that the delay can contribute to deaths. It puts the modern case fatality rate at 10 to 20 percent based on earlier case series. In a recent Northern California cluster of Amanita poisonings from foraged mushrooms, 39 cases were reported to poison control between November 2025 and March 2026. Of those, 3 patients needed liver transplants and 4 died. That cluster involved Amanita, not Galerina, but amatoxins are the same family of toxins.

The California Department of Public Health describes the same trap: early symptoms can go away within a day, and that brief improvement can be deceptive, because serious liver damage can still develop 48 to 96 hours after eating.

What is alpha-proamanitin, and is it less dangerous?

This is the most interesting chemistry in the paper. The main amatoxin in Galerina indica was one nobody had seen in a mushroom before. The team named it alpha-proamanitin. It is built like alpha-amanitin, the most common deadly amatoxin, except that it lacks a single hydroxyl group on a proline residue. Alpha-amanitin itself was not detected in any Galerina indica sample.

The authors reason that this missing group could make alpha-proamanitin a weaker blocker of RNA polymerase II, based on lab studies of related amatoxins. They also say amatoxins missing that group "remain toxic." And they float a hypothesis that picking up psilocybin genes may have taken pressure off the lineage to make the stronger toxin.

Please do not read "possibly less potent" as "safe." Nothing in the sources I read measures the toxicity of alpha-proamanitin in people. It is a structural argument and a hypothesis. The only safe reading is that this mushroom carries amatoxins.

Can you tell it apart from other mushrooms by looking?

No, and the researchers say so. Smith said it is "hard to differentiate from others, even for a trained expert." The team needed DNA sequencing of three genetic regions to place the species, plus mass spectrometry to confirm what was in it.

The blue bruising is a good example of why looks fail. In this case, a mushroom that stains blue turned out to carry a deadly toxin as well as psilocybin. Bruising color, size, habitat and cap shape are clues for a mycologist with a microscope. They are not a safety test for someone standing in a yard.

The Children's Hospital of Philadelphia poison center gives the same advice for every wild mushroom: the close similarities make it hard for anyone but a trained expert to tell them apart, so the best prevention is to assume that no wild mushrooms are safe to eat.

How did one mushroom end up with both?

Through horizontal gene transfer, which is when genes move between unrelated species instead of passing from parent to offspring. Live Science calls it widespread among fungi. The preprint says both the psilocybin and amatoxin pathways were already known to have moved between distantly related fungi.

The team's reading is that the Galerina indica lineage already made amatoxins, and later picked up the psilocybin gene cluster. The psilocybin genes sit in a different order than in other psilocybin mushrooms, which suggests the cluster was rearranged after it arrived. Smith estimated the transfer at about 15.5 million years ago. Kunik noted it may have happened in two separate events, possibly as recently as 6 million years ago, and that the family tree needs more specimens to settle it.

Why would a mushroom carry both? Nobody knows yet. One hypothesis is defense: an animal that eats mushrooms would have a hard time evolving resistance to two unrelated chemicals. Kunik called that a speculation, and the UF release says amatoxins may help protect mushrooms from animals that eat them but their exact role is not clear.

Does this change anything for people who pick wild mushrooms?

It breaks a tidy sorting rule. The University of Florida release opens by noting that mushrooms seemed to split into two camps, magic and poisonous. Galerina indica sits in both, and it is a small, brown, easy-to-miss species, exactly the kind a person could gather by mistake.

Federal law lists psilocybin and psilocin as Schedule I. For the legal picture on mushrooms and spores, see our dated state by state summary in Are mushroom spores legal in the United States?. Nothing here is advice to go looking for any mushroom.

For anyone who forages for food, the practical rules are old and still right:

  • Do not eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has identified that exact mushroom in person. Photos in an app are not enough.
  • Cooking, drying and freezing do not remove amatoxins.
  • Early stomach symptoms that clear up are not a sign you are fine.

What should you do after eating an unknown wild mushroom?

Act right away, before any symptoms.

  • In the United States, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222. Call 911 if the person is very sick.
  • Do not wait for symptoms. Amatoxin poisoning is dangerous because the first symptoms come late.
  • Keep a sample of what was eaten if you can, bagged and clearly marked as poison, so a mycologist can identify it. A photo of the mushroom and where it grew also helps.
  • Tell the hospital exactly what was eaten and when, even if the person feels better.

The North American Mycological Association says the same: if you suspect someone has eaten an amatoxin mushroom, do not wait for symptoms to appear.

Why do known species in a controlled chamber make more sense than foraging?

Because the biggest risk in this story is not knowing what you have. Foraging asks you to identify a mushroom from a few clues, and this paper shows that even experts needed a lab for it.

FAQ

Is the dark magic mushroom the same as a magic mushroom?

No. Magic mushrooms usually means species that make psilocybin, mostly in the genus Psilocybe. The dark magic mushroom is Galerina indica, a different genus. It makes psilocybin and also amatoxins, which are the toxins that make death caps deadly.

Is Galerina indica really the first mushroom with both compounds?

The researchers say so. Study first author Andrew Kunik told Live Science that no mushroom species in any group had been known to produce both at the same time. The preprint adds that no phylogenetically placed Galerina species had been reported to produce psilocybin before this study.

How deadly are amatoxins?

Amatoxin poisoning has a modern case fatality rate of 10 to 20 percent, according to the CDC. The North American Mycological Association puts it at about 10 percent in the United States and Canada with good medical care and about 50 percent without prompt treatment. Cooking does not remove the toxins.

Has anyone been poisoned by Galerina indica?

None of the sources I read describe a poisoning by this species. Its relatives in the Galerina marginata group, sometimes called funeral bells, are well documented as deadly.

Is alpha-proamanitin safer than alpha-amanitin?

Nobody knows. The authors argue from structure that it may be a weaker blocker of RNA polymerase II, but they also say amatoxins like it remain toxic, and no toxicity data for people are reported.

What should I do if I think someone ate a poisonous mushroom?

Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 in the United States right away, or 911 if they are very sick. Do not wait for symptoms, because amatoxin symptoms are delayed.

Where is the original study?

It is Kunik et al., "The dark magic mushroom co-produces amatoxins and psilocybin," Current Biology, 2026. The DOI is 10.1016/j.cub.2026.10.041. An open preprint is on bioRxiv and PubMed Central.
