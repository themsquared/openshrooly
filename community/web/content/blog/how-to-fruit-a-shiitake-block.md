## What does a shiitake block need to fruit?

Time, a brown skin, and a cold, wet signal. Shiitake is not like oyster, where you cut a hole in a white bag and get mushrooms in a week. A white shiitake block is not ready. It has to sit under low light until the outside turns brown and tough, and then it needs a jolt of cold water or cold air before it pins.

These are the stages and numbers from our [shiitake profile](/profiles/shiitake):

| | Colonization | Browning | Cold shock | Fruiting |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Days | 42 | 21 | 1 | 10 |
| Temperature | 24 °C (75 °F) | 21 °C (70 °F) | 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F), in a fridge | 16 °C (61 °F) |
| Humidity | 88% | 80% | 90% | 85% |
| Light | Dark | 12 h a day, about 200 lux, blue | Dark | 12 h a day, about 90 lux, blue |
| Chamber fan | 15 s every 60 min | 20 s every 45 min | 15 s every 60 min | 35 s every 12 min |

That is 63 days of colonizing and browning before the shock, and 74 days from spawn to the end of the first flush. Plug your start date into the [grow timeline calculator](/tools/grow-timeline) to get calendar dates for each stage.

If your block came from a kit already brown and firm, it is past the browning stage. Start at the soak.

## Why does a shiitake block have to turn brown first?

The brown coat is the block's bark. A 2024 study from Korea's National Institute of Forest Science describes what it does: once browning is complete, the block resists contamination even in open air and loses less moisture, which helps the mushrooms develop. If browning is incomplete, the block is treated as contaminated and a good crop is not expected ([Kim et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/12298093.2024.2430052)).

The brown film is triggered by light. A 2019 genetics study calls light induced browning of the mycelium an important step for shiitake yield and quality ([Yoo et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-019-5509-4)). That is why our profile keeps colonization dark and turns the light on for the browning rest.

You do not need much light for it. Kim et al. cite earlier work that found high browning efficiency at 200 lux, whatever the light source, with blue LED browning best. A 2018 study using LED light to brown sawdust blocks at 20 °C for 60 days got the best browning at a low 1.5 µmol/m²/s of red and blue light. Its strongest settings, 20.5 µmol/m²/s LED and 300 lux fluorescent, browned less ([Koo et al. 2018](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A348305)). So our profile uses 12 hours a day at about 200 lux and stops there. More light is not better for browning.

## How long should a shiitake block brown?

Longer than you want to wait. Our profile gives the block 42 days dark and 21 days under light, 63 days in all, and the profile note says to wait for full white and then browning and not to fruit early. Treat 63 days as the minimum and go by the block, not the calendar.

The research backs patience. In a Penn State study, sawdust blocks given a 116 day spawn run produced two to three times the yield (biological efficiency) of blocks given 58 days, and the mushrooms were generally larger ([Royse 1985](https://doi.org/10.1080/00275514.1985.12025163)). Kim et al. put the usual browning culture for sawdust shiitake at 100 to 150 days. In their own trial, blocks got 60 days dark and then 40 or 60 days of light, and 40 days under blue light beat white light on yield in both strains tested ([Kim et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/12298093.2024.2430052)).

## How do you know a shiitake block is ready to fruit?

A 2024 study tracked shiitake blocks through five stages before it fruited them ([El Sebaaly et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0309787)):

1. Mycelium run: the substrate is fully covered by a thin layer of white.
2. Coat formation: the white sheet hardens over the whole surface.
3. Bumps: clumps of mycelium form a popcorn texture.
4. Browning: the outside turns dark brown and dry.
5. Coat hardening: the brown coat hardens.

They waited for the end of stage 5 before shocking. Popcorn bumps alone mean the block is getting close, not that it is ready. When most of the block is brown and the skin feels firm and leathery, it is time.

## Step by step: fruiting a shiitake block

1. Colonize in the dark. Keep the bag closed at around 24 °C (75 °F) until it is fully white. Our profile allows 42 days.
2. Brown it under low light. Leave the bag closed, drop to around 21 °C (70 °F), and give it 12 hours a day of low light, about 200 lux. Our profile runs this rest at 80% humidity, a bit drier than colonization, for 21 days or longer.
3. Check for a firm brown skin. Wait until the bumps have browned and the coat has hardened. White patches mean it needs more time.
4. Take it out of the bag. Cut or peel the plastic off. Two of the block studies below say they opened or peeled the bag before the cold soak.
5. Soak or chill it. If the block feels light, submerge it in cold water with a weight on top for up to 24 hours. If it still feels heavy and wet, a night in the fridge at 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F) for 12 to 24 hours does the same job. The next two sections cover timing.
6. Fruit it cool and damp. Move it to around 16 °C (61 °F) and 85% humidity with 12 hours of low light and steady fresh air. Mist the air, not the caps.
7. Watch for pins in 2 to 3 days. In the 2024 study, pins showed 2 to 3 days after the soak and mushrooms reached full size 4 to 5 days after that.
8. Harvest at 50 to 75% open. Pick while the gill edges are still rolled under. Twist from the base or cut flush with the block.
9. Rest it, then repeat. Give the block about 2 weeks to recover, then soak again for the next flush.

## How long should you soak a shiitake block?

Up to 24 hours in cold water is what the block studies used. Here is what each one did:

| Study | Soak | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sawdust blocks ([Sabota et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.21273/horttech.14.3.0393)) | 24 h in tap water, weighted down, six soakings over the season | Plain water was the control. Adding sugar to the soak lowered yield |
| Lebanese University, 1 kg bags ([El Sebaaly et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0309787)) | 24 h in ice, bag removed | Pins in 2 to 3 days, full size 4 to 5 days later |
| Bag logs ([Annepu et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3329/bjb.v48i1.47431)) | 15 min in ice cold water, 4 to 6 °C (39 to 43 °F), bag peeled | Same cold soak repeated for the second and third flush |

The soak does two jobs: it wets the block and it chills it. A 15 minute ice cold dip is mostly a cold signal. A 24 hour soak also gives the block time to take up water.

Ohio State Extension gives the rule growers use for shiitake logs: the closer the log temperature is to the water temperature, the longer the soak. Their example is 6 to 24 hours in summer and 2 to 3 days in spring or fall ([Ohio State Extension 2022](https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/f-0042)). That is for logs. Every block study above stayed at 24 hours or less, so use that as your ceiling for a block. Do not add sugar to the soak water. In the 2004 trial, sucrose in the soak cut mushroom numbers and yield in both strains.

## Does shiitake need a cold shock?

It needs a change, and cold is the most reliable one. Ohio State says temperature and moisture changes trigger shiitake to fruit ([Ohio State Extension 2022](https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/f-0042)). NCAT's ATTRA guide, a USDA funded grower program, says a dropping temperature triggers it, and that growers soak logs to start fruiting ([NCAT ATTRA 2026](https://www.ncat.org/publication/mushroom-cultivation/)).

Our profile's cold shock is 12 to 24 hours at 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F), a range the profile notes credit to Stamets. A fruiting chamber cannot get that cold, so if you run the profile on a Shroom Labs chamber, this one stage happens in your fridge. Put the block in, then apply the fruiting stage when it comes back out. A cold water soak from the tap or with ice in it gives the same signal if your fridge is full.

Strain matters here. NCAT says warm weather strains fruit best with highs around 70 to 80 °F (21 to 27 °C), cool weather strains at roughly 50 to 65 °F (10 to 18 °C), and wide range strains respond well to forced fruiting. Check the temperature notes from whoever sold you the block. Our profile is a warm weather block strain.

## What temperature, humidity, light and fresh air does shiitake need to fruit?

Cool, damp, lit, and well aired. Our profile fruits at 16 °C (61 °F) with warnings below 12 °C (54 °F) and above 21 °C (70 °F). The published numbers line up:

- Temperature. NCAT gives 60 to 70 °F (15 to 21 °C) for shiitake fruiting. Ohio State gives 55 to 65 °F (13 to 18 °C) for pinning. The 2024 block study fruited at 16 °C (61 °F), and Kim et al. at 18 °C (64 °F).
- Humidity. Our profile holds 85%. Ohio State asks for at least 85% while pinning, and NCAT gives 85 to 95% for fruiting in general. The block studies ranged from 80% (Kim et al.) to above 90% (El Sebaaly et al.).
- Light. Light is required for normal shiitake fruiting, and only pigmented pins went on to expand in the classic lab work ([Leatham and Stahmann 1987](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0007-1536%2887%2980180-8)). Ohio State says enough light to read a newspaper at arm's length is enough. Our profile runs 12 hours a day at about 90 lux as a labeled default, because no shiitake specific fruiting lux was found for the chamber. The 2024 block study used 2,000 lux, so brighter room light is fine. [Do mushrooms need light?](/blog/do-mushrooms-need-light) has the numbers for other species.
- Fresh air. Our profile jumps from a 20 second fan run every 45 minutes while browning to 35 seconds every 12 minutes while fruiting. Kim et al. held carbon dioxide at 1,000 ppm in their fruiting room. A block on an open shelf gets enough. A block in a closed tote does not.

Our two other shiitake profiles push the cap on purpose. The [donko profile](/profiles/shiitake-donko) pins for 6 days at 13 °C (55 °F) and 95%, then fruits for 14 days at 15 °C (59 °F) and 75% for a thick, rounded cap. The [flower cap profile](/profiles/shiitake-flower-cap) fruits at 14 °C (57 °F) and only 60% so the dry air splits the cap skin into a white pattern. Neither is the place to start. Cracked caps on a regular grow mean the air is too dry, not that you have made flower shiitake. See [dry, cracked mushroom caps](/guides/dry-cracked-mushroom-caps).

## When do you harvest shiitake?

When the cap is 50 to 75% open and the gill edges are still rolled under. Ohio State says picking at 100% open shortens shelf life, delays the next flush and invites pests. Twist the mushroom off by the stem, or cut it flush with a sharp knife, and touch only the stem because bruised caps and gills discolor fast ([Ohio State Extension 2022](https://ohioline.osu.edu/factsheet/f-0042)).

Cool them to 33 to 35 °F (about 1 to 2 °C) as fast as you can, in a basket or paper bag, not a plastic bag. Ohio State notes frost free fridges tend to dry mushrooms out.

## How do you get a second flush from a shiitake block?

Rest it, then soak it again. After the first harvest, the 2024 block study kept its blocks in the dark at 22 °C (72 °F) and 60% humidity for two weeks, then soaked them in ice for another 24 hours ([El Sebaaly et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0309787)). NCAT gives 7 to 14 days of rest between flushes in general and notes later flushes give diminishing returns. The 2004 trial soaked its blocks six times in one season ([Sabota et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.21273/horttech.14.3.0393)).

The rest matters because the block just spent its stored energy and a lot of water. Soaking it again the day after harvest skips the recovery. Expect the second flush to be smaller. In the 2024 study, every substrate gave fewer mushrooms on the second harvest than the first.

## Why is my shiitake block not fruiting?

Most stalled shiitake blocks are one of these:

- It is not brown yet. A white or patchy block was shocked too early. Put it back under 12 hours of low light and wait.
- It never got a signal. A block kept warm with no soak and no cold spell can sit for weeks. Soak or chill it.
- The room is too warm for the strain. A cool weather strain in a 75 °F (24 °C) room will sit. Check the strain notes.
- It dried out. A light, hard block needs a long soak.
- It is in the dark or sealed in a tote with no air.
- It was soaked again right after a harvest with no rest.

## FAQ

### How long does it take a shiitake block to fruit?

About 9 weeks before the first shock on our profile, 42 days to colonize and 21 days to brown. Kim et al. put the usual browning culture for sawdust shiitake at 100 to 150 days. After the shock, expect pins in 2 to 3 days and mushrooms 4 to 5 days later.

### How long do you soak a shiitake block?

Up to 24 hours in cold water, weighted under. Two of the block studies soaked for 24 hours, and a third did a 15 minute dip in water at 4 to 6 °C (39 to 43 °F).

### Can I put a shiitake block in the fridge to shock it?

Yes. Our profile uses 12 to 24 hours at 5 to 10 °C (41 to 50 °F), which is what a fridge does. Bring it straight out to cool, damp, lit fruiting conditions afterward.

### Does a shiitake block need light?

Yes, twice. Low light, about 200 lux for 12 hours a day, browns the skin, and light is needed again for normal fruiting.

### Why are my shiitake caps cracking?

Dry air. Raise the humidity toward 85% and mist the air, not the caps. Our flower cap profile does this on purpose at 60% humidity.
