## How do you tell green mold from healthy mycelium in a photo?

Look at color first, then texture, then how it changes over a day or two. Our [contamination guide](/guides/mushroom-contamination) covers every kind of contamination. This post zooms in on the green and blue-green ones, because they cause the most confusion and they are the ones that hide inside white growth at the start.

| What you see in the photo | Most likely | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Even white, cottony or ropey growth across the block | Healthy mycelium | Nothing. Keep going. |
| A denser, brighter white patch that looks different from the rest | Could be early Trichoderma, could be healthy | Photograph it every 24 h |
| White patch with a green center or green edges | Trichoderma making spores | Bag and remove the block |
| Dark forest green, powdery, spreading | Trichoderma | Bag and remove the block |
| Small blue-green spots | Often Penicillium, can be other molds | Remove if opened, isolate if sealed |
| A brown line where white growth meets a patch | Mushroom mycelium reacting to a mold | Treat it as contaminated |
| Brown, even, leathery skin on a shiitake block | Normal shiitake browning | Nothing. This is what you want. |
| Blue-gray pins or caps on a blue oyster | Normal pigment | Nothing |
| Yellow or amber drops on white mycelium | Normal liquid from the mycelium | Nothing |

Two lab facts explain why this table needs a time column. Penn State Extension describes green mold as dense white growth that is followed by heavy green sporulation. A 2024 study of an oyster farm said the same thing: at first the mold could not be told apart from the oyster mycelium, because both were dense and white, and the green came later ([Ajis, Tan and Chai 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.funbio.2024.09.005)). One photo cannot tell them apart. Two photos a day apart often can.

## What does Trichoderma look like at each stage?

Trichoderma is a genus, not one mold. On oyster mushrooms the usual culprits are _Trichoderma pleurotum_ and _T. pleuroticola_, which were confirmed on oyster farms in Europe, Iran and South Korea, and _T. pleuroticola_ also lives in soil and wood in the United States and Canada ([Komoń-Zelazowska et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1128/AEM.01059-07)). On shiitake, a two year survey of contaminated substrate in China turned up 14 different Trichoderma species from 150 samples ([Cao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1288585)).

What they share is a pattern you can see:

1. **White and dense.** A patch that is thicker or fluffier than the mycelium around it.
2. **Green edges or a green center.** Penn State's IPM guide for mushroom farms says the patch starts out gray, turns white along the edges and goes green as it sporulates.
3. **Dark green powder.** The patch spreads and the spores dust off if the block is bumped.

Not every Trichoderma is green on day one. In a Korean extension study, _T. harzianum_ colonies turned bright green, _T. cf. virens_ was yellowish green, and _T. longibrachiatum_ was yellow on lab agar ([Choi et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.4489/MYCO.2003.31.3.139)). That is one more reason to judge by change over time, not a single color.

## What is the brown line between the mold and the white mycelium?

That line is the mushroom fighting back, and it is a warning sign. Penn State notes browning of button mushroom mycelium and small pins breaking down at the advancing edge of green mold. In lab plates of shiitake against Trichoderma, the shiitake put out more brown substances and formed brown lines where the two met ([Cao et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2023.1288585)). The 2026 oyster lab work showed the same kind of discoloration where Trichoderma overgrew the mushroom ([Shan et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.4014/jmb.2605.05010)).

So a white block with a sharp brown border around one patch is not bruising. Treat it as contaminated, even if the green has not shown yet.

## Is brown or blue on my block ever normal?

Yes, and these are the three that fool people most:

- **Shiitake turns brown on purpose.** Our [shiitake profile](/profiles/shiitake) runs 42 days of colonization and then 21 days of browning at 21 °C, because the block forms a protective brown skin before it fruits. In a Japanese sawdust study, healthy shiitake bags were fully white by day 26 and covered in brown pigmented mycelium after day 61 ([Terashima, Igusa and Ohga 2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/s102670200041)). Even, leathery brown is good. Fuzzy green on top of the brown is not.
- **Blue oyster really is blue.** Our [blue oyster profile](/profiles/blue-oyster) colonizes at 22 °C and pins at 15 °C, and notes that the deep color holds better below 18 °C. Blue-gray pins with a cap and stem shape are the crop. Blue-green powder on flat substrate is mold.
- **Yellow drops are fine.** Amber liquid on white mycelium is normal and is covered in the [contamination guide](/guides/mushroom-contamination).

The test for all three is shape. Mushrooms and healthy mycelium have structure: strands, ropes, pins, caps. Mold on the surface is a flat, powdery mat with no structure.

## Is blue-green mold the same as green mold?

Not always. When Thai researchers collected green spore molds from shiitake logs and sawdust bags in five production houses, they got 43 isolates: 62.8% Trichoderma, 20.9% Gliocladium, 9.3% Penicillium and 7.0% Aspergillus ([Pukahuta et al. 2000](https://li01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/anres/article/view/240391)). Many blue-green spots are Penicillium, but you cannot confirm the genus from a phone photo.

The difference matters, at least on shiitake. In that Japanese study, bags infected with _Penicillium brevicompactum_ at the same time as spawning showed spores by day 6, but by day 26 the shiitake had grown over them, and yield and mushroom size were no different from clean bags, though some of those bags gave more misshapen mushrooms. Bags infected with _T. harzianum_ on any of the four test days were covered in spores and fatally damaged ([Terashima, Igusa and Ohga 2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/s102670200041)). The University of Florida grow guide lists blue, green, black and yellow spots all as signs of contamination, and calls out Trichoderma for its rapid greenish growth ([Liao et al., UF/IFAS SS662](https://doi.org/10.32473/edis-ss662-2018)).

## What should you do with a block that has green mold?

Bag it and get it out. The steps for safe removal and cleanup are in the [contamination guide](/guides/mushroom-contamination), and we will not repeat them here. Three points that are specific to green mold:

- **Do not scrape it off.** The white stage you cannot see is often bigger than the green you can. Penn State calls out people and equipment as the main way green mold moves around a farm. A spoon that touched the patch is now carrying it.
- **Do not save the fruit for dinner.** The UF guide says partially contaminated bags may still give one flush. That is a farm yield note, not a food safety pass. On a home kit, mushrooms from a moldy block are not worth the risk.
- **Look at the block's neighbors.** A 2024 study found water was the main source of green mold on the oyster farm it studied, followed by air in the growing house ([Ajis, Tan and Chai 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.funbio.2024.09.005)). If you mist several blocks from one bottle, check every block that bottle touched.

## Can you save a block with a small blue-green spot?

Sometimes, if the bag is still sealed. Here is the one case where we wait instead of tossing:

- The bag has never been opened and is still colonizing.
- The spot is small, blue-green, and not a dense white patch going green.
- There is no brown border around it.

Move that bag away from your other grows, leave it sealed, and photograph it every 24 hours. If white mycelium grows over the spot, as the Penicillium did on shiitake in the Japanese study, carry on. If the spot grows, turns dark green, or gets a brown border, it is done. Once a bag is open or fruiting, skip the wait and remove it.

## Why does green mold show up in the first place?

Heat is the big one. In a Korean extension study, Trichoderma isolates from oyster farms grew best at 27 to 30 °C, and _T. longibrachiatum_ could still grow at 37 °C while growing 30 to 40 times faster than the oyster mycelium ([Choi et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.4489/MYCO.2003.31.3.139)). A University of Szeged dissertation on mushroom Trichoderma summarizes work showing a substrate bag can heat itself to 30 °C during spawn run from the mycelium's own metabolism, that Trichoderma growth peaked at 30 °C and stopped at 15 °C, and that Trichoderma grew three times faster than oyster mycelium at 25 °C ([Hatvani 2008](https://www2.sci.u-szeged.hu/fokozatok/PDF/Hatvani_Lorant/dissertation_Hatvani_L.pdf)).

Compare that with the colonization temperatures in our own profiles:

| Profile | Colonization temperature | Days |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Blue oyster](/profiles/blue-oyster) | 22 °C | 12 |
| [Lion's mane](/profiles/lions-mane) | 22 °C | 16 |
| [Pearl oyster](/profiles/pearl-oyster) | 24 °C | 14 |
| [Shiitake](/profiles/shiitake) | 24 °C | 42 |
| [Golden oyster](/profiles/golden-oyster) | 25 °C | 12 |
| [Pink oyster](/profiles/pink-oyster) | 27 °C | 10 |

Most of them sit a few degrees under the Trichoderma sweet spot. A block in a warm cupboard, or one sitting on top of a fridge, can easily drift into it. Pink oyster colonizes right at the bottom of that range, which is one reason a fast, clean spawn run matters so much for it. Shiitake spends six weeks colonizing, so a small mistake has a long time to grow.

The other cause surprises people: clean substrate can be easier for Trichoderma to take over. An Argentine study sprayed Trichoderma spores onto straw, grain and sawdust after different treatments and then spawned them with oyster mushroom. Trichoderma could not grow on substrate that was not sterilized, sterilized substrate favored it, and growing alongside the mushroom helped it. Hot water at 60 °C for 30 minutes or 36 hours in alkaline lime water cut contamination best ([Colavolpe, Jaramillo Mejía and Albertó 2014](https://doi.org/10.1590/S1517-83822014000400017)). Sterile substrate has no other microbes to defend it, so one spore that lands at spawning has the place to itself.

## How do you stop green mold next time?

- **Keep colonization at the low end of the profile.** Use the profile temperature, not "warm." If the room runs warm, check that the block is not heating itself above that.
- **Spawn fast and clean.** The UF guide says to inoculate pasteurized straw as soon as it has drained and cooled, and to clean the work surface and room first. It pasteurizes straw at 165 °F (74 °C) for 1 to 2 hours and spawns at 5 to 10%.
- **Leave sterile bags sealed until they are white all the way through.** Sterile substrate has no backup defense, so every opening before full colonization is a risk.
- **Do not share tools or water between a suspect block and clean ones.** Penn State and the 2024 farm study both point at people, equipment and water as the way green mold moves.
- **Drop the temperature for fruiting.** The Hatvani dissertation summarizes advice to keep the room at 15 to 18 °C after spawn run to slow green mold. Our pearl and king oyster profiles already fruit in that range, and blue oyster a little below it.
- **Retire spent blocks.** A block that has given its flushes has little defense left. The [contamination guide](/guides/mushroom-contamination) covers late mold on spent blocks.

## How to check a suspicious patch with photos

1. Take a photo in daylight or under the same white light, with the whole block and the patch in frame.
2. Take a close-up of the patch from about 10 cm (4 in), and put a coin or ruler next to the bag so you can judge size later.
3. Do not open the bag, press it, or wipe the patch.
4. Take the same two photos from the same angle 24 hours later, and again at 48 hours.
5. Compare them side by side. Spreading green, a new green center, or a brown border means Trichoderma is likely. Bag and remove the block.
6. If it stays white and blends into the rest, it was mycelium. If you still are not sure, post the photo set on the [grow feed](/feed) for a second opinion.

Most of the time the answer is in the 24 hour photo. If your kit is doing nothing at all rather than growing something odd, start with [mushroom grow kit not growing](/guides/mushroom-grow-kit-not-growing) instead.

## FAQ

### Is green mold on a mushroom block Trichoderma?

Usually. In a survey of green spore molds from shiitake houses, 62.8% of isolates were Trichoderma. The rest were Gliocladium, Penicillium and Aspergillus. You cannot confirm the genus at home, but the handling is the same: bag and remove the block.

### Does Trichoderma start out white?

Yes. Penn State describes green mold as dense white growth followed by green sporulation, and a 2024 oyster farm study said the early mold could not be told apart from oyster mycelium. Photograph any unusual white patch every 24 hours.

### What temperature does green mold like?

Trichoderma isolates from oyster farms grew best at 27 to 30 °C (81 to 86 °F) in a Korean study. Work summarized in a University of Szeged dissertation found it stopped growing at 15 °C. Keeping colonization near 22 to 24 °C and fruiting cooler helps.

### Can mushrooms beat green mold on their own?

Rarely. In a Japanese shiitake study, bags infected with Trichoderma harzianum were fatally damaged no matter when the infection happened. Penicillium was overgrown by the shiitake. Do not count on a block winning against green mold.

### Is blue-green mold Penicillium?

Often, but you cannot confirm it from a photo. In the shiitake house survey, Penicillium was 9.3% of green spore isolates and Trichoderma was 62.8%, so treat any blue-green spot on an opened block as contamination.

### Why is my shiitake block turning brown?

That is normal. Shiitake forms a protective brown skin before fruiting. Our shiitake profile gives it 21 days of browning at 21 °C after colonization. Even brown is good. Green fuzz on top of it is mold.
