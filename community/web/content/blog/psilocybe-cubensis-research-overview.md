## Short answer

Psilocybe cubensis is the best known psilocybin mushroom and one of the most studied. Its name, its family, its wild range and the history of its cultivars have all been revised by DNA work in the last few years, so a lot of what gets repeated online is out of date.

This post is a research summary. Every claim below points to a taxonomy database, a government source or a peer reviewed paper with a DOI. It is not a guide to finding, growing or using any mushroom, and it makes no health claims. Shroom Labs does not sell spores, cultures or psilocybin products.

## How is Psilocybe cubensis classified?

The species was first published in 1906 by Franklin Sumner Earle as Stropharia cubensis, in volume 1 of the annual report of Cuba's Estación Central Agronómica, page 240. The type came from Cuba. NCBI Taxonomy lists the holotype as New York Botanical Garden herbarium specimen NY 761563.

Rolf Singer moved it to Psilocybe in 1948, in Sydowia volume 2, page 37. That combination, Psilocybe cubensis (Earle) Singer, is the current name in Index Fungorum.

Older books put the species in Strophariaceae, which is where Index Fungorum still files the original name Stropharia cubensis. If you read an older paper that says Strophariaceae, it is the same mushroom.

### Synonyms

Kew's species record, which takes its names from Index Fungorum, lists ten synonyms. The ones you are most likely to see in older literature:

- Stropharia cubensis Earle, the original 1906 name
- Stropharia cyanescens Murrill
- Naematoloma caerulescens Pat.
- Hypholoma caerulescens (Pat.) Sacc. and Trotter
- Psilocybe cubensis var. caerulescens (Pat.) Singer and A.H. Sm.
- Psilocybe cubensis var. cyanescens (Murrill) Singer and A.H. Sm.
- Psilocybe fasciata Hongo

### Where it sits in the genus

A 2024 PNAS study by Bradshaw and colleagues sequenced 71 fungarium specimens, 23 of them type specimens, and built a tree from 2,983 single copy gene families. They put the genus at about 165 species, with the stem lineage arising about 67 million years ago. They also found the psilocybin genes come in two different orders that match a deep split in the genus. The original order was first reported from P. cubensis and P. serbica.

## Where does Psilocybe cubensis grow wild?

On dung. Both major recent papers describe it as a dung specialist tied to livestock, mostly cattle. McTaggart and colleagues (2023) call livestock dung its niche. Bradshaw and colleagues (2026) describe it as a widespread dung specialist and say its specialization on domesticated cattle dung likely traces back to an ancestor that lived on the dung of large wild herbivores.

The 2026 study's climate models place its likely historical range across the Americas, Southeast Asia and Australasia. The authors say they know of no authenticated record of P. cubensis from Africa. African collections once filed under that name turned out to be a different species (see below).

Australia is the best studied population. McTaggart and colleagues sequenced 38 Australian isolates and found a population that went through a sharp bottleneck when it was introduced, then recovered and kept relatively high genetic diversity. They describe it as naturalized, spread with cattle, and note it has caused accidental poisonings there since the 1940s.

## Where did Psilocybe cubensis come from?

The common story is that it reached the Americas with cattle brought from Africa and Europe around 1500 CE. The 2026 Proceedings B paper by Bradshaw and colleagues tested that by sequencing type specimens of every known and accessible African Psilocybe species.

What they found:

- African specimens that had been called P. cubensis are a separate species, which they named Psilocybe ochraceocentrata. It is the closest known wild relative of P. cubensis.
- The two species last shared a common ancestor about 1.5 million years ago, with a 95% range of about 710,000 to 2.55 million years. That is long before cattle were domesticated.
- Both species live on the dung of large herbivores.

So the cattle story may explain how P. cubensis moved around in the last 500 years, but it does not explain where the lineage started. The authors frame their range maps as hypotheses to test, not settled history.

## What does the research document about its chemistry?

Psilocybin was first isolated and named in 1958 by Albert Hofmann and colleagues, from a different species, Psilocybe mexicana. Two later papers worked out how P. cubensis makes it and why it turns blue.

- **Biosynthesis.** Fricke, Blei and Hoffmeister (2017) characterized four enzymes, PsiD, PsiK, PsiM and PsiH, and made psilocybin enzymatically from 4-hydroxy-L-tryptophan using three of them.
- **The blue bruise.** Lenz and colleagues (published online in 2019) found two P. cubensis enzymes behind the blue color on damaged tissue. PsiP removes the phosphate from psilocybin to give psilocin, and PsiL oxidizes psilocin. The oxidized units link into chains of roughly 3 to 13 units, and those are the blue products.
- **Genome.** McKernan and colleagues (2021) published a draft reference genome of 46.6 million base pairs, 46% GC, in 32 contigs. The psilocybin gene cluster sits on a single 3.2 million base pair contig.

I am leaving out potency numbers on purpose. They vary between samples, they are not what this post is about, and Shroom Labs does not publish dosing information.

## What do Golden Teacher, B+ and Penis Envy mean genetically?

They are cultivar names used in trade, not species. All three of our profiles list the species as Psilocybe cubensis.

The best data on what those names mean comes from McTaggart and colleagues (2023), who compared the 38 Australian genomes with 86 genomes of commercial cultivars, 124 in total. Their results:

- The cultivars sorted into five groups: Blue Magnolia (20 genomes), Golden Teacher (37), Luminous (15), Malabar (8) and P-envy (6). B+ is not one of the named groups.
- Cultivars show the marks of selfing and clonal propagation: low genetic diversity, low heterozygosity and few mating type alleles.
- Golden Teacher and P-envy were the least heterozygous groups. Golden Teacher had a mean inbreeding coefficient of 0.784 and P-envy 0.811, where 1 means completely inbred.
- The Australian population carried 13 HD and 9 PR mating type alleles. Golden Teacher carried 3 and 5, and P-envy 2 and 5.
- Most cultivars were founded from related populations, and the psilocybin gene alleles are identical across most of them.

In plain terms, a lot of differently named cultivars are close relatives. For the profiles on this site, read the cultivar name as a label, not a pedigree. ## FAQ

### What family is Psilocybe cubensis in?

Index Fungorum and NCBI Taxonomy both place it in Agrocybaceae, order Agaricales, as of 2026-11-23. Older sources say Strophariaceae, which is where the original 1906 name Stropharia cubensis was filed.

### Who named Psilocybe cubensis?

Franklin Sumner Earle described it from Cuba in 1906 as Stropharia cubensis. Rolf Singer transferred it to Psilocybe in 1948, which is why the full name is Psilocybe cubensis (Earle) Singer.

### Where is Psilocybe cubensis native?

That is still open. It grows on cattle and other large herbivore dung in warm parts of the Americas, Southeast Asia and Australasia. A 2026 study found its closest wild relative in sub-Saharan Africa and dated their split to about 1.5 million years ago, and the same study reports no authenticated African records of P. cubensis itself.

### Are Golden Teacher and Penis Envy different species?

No. Both are cultivars of P. cubensis. A 2023 genome study found them among the least genetically diverse cultivar groups, with signs of long term inbreeding and clonal propagation.

### Why does Psilocybe cubensis bruise blue?

Damaged tissue starts an enzyme cascade. PsiP turns psilocybin into psilocin and PsiL oxidizes psilocin, which then links into blue chains, according to Lenz and colleagues (2019).

### Is Psilocybe cubensis legal?

Psilocybin and psilocin are Schedule I under US federal law and the 1971 UN Convention. A few places run regulated programs, listed in the table above with sources. Check the current law where you live.
